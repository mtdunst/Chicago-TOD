Questions

Is this a forecast of what will be built?

No. It estimates how many homes could fit under stated density targets. What actually gets built depends on the market, financing, ownership, and public decisions. Think of it as the size of the opportunity.

Does this mean buildings would be torn down?

The "Build on open land first" scenario counts only vacant lots and surface parking, so it involves no demolition. Other scenarios include low-value buildings that could be replaced over decades, as owners choose. Nothing here requires anyone to sell or redevelop.

Who owns this land?

Most sites are privately owned. Public sites belong to the City, Cook County, the Cook County Land Bank, transit agencies, Chicago Public Schools, the Chicago Housing Authority, and others. Owner names come from Cook County Assessor records; click any site on the map to see its owner.

How sure are these numbers?

Every figure is rounded. Existing building sizes come from County Assessor records where available. Where they aren't, mostly on public land, building size is estimated from the City's 2021 building footprints. Look for these markers:

  • Solid based on County records
  • Likely estimated from building footprints, or land with no buildings
  • Rough estimated with the number of stories filled in from similar buildings
What do FAR, planned development, and as-of-right mean?

FAR is building size relative to the lot: a two-story building covering its whole lot has a FAR of 2. A planned development is a custom zoning approval for a large site. As-of-right means allowed under today's zoning with no special approval.

About

Sources: Cook County Assessor (property values, owners, building characteristics, commercial valuations); City of Chicago (zoning, building footprints, City-owned land, ward boundaries); CTA and Metra schedule data; U.S. Census Bureau (2020 Census housing counts, American Community Survey income and tenure).

Limitations: building footprints date to about 2021, so newer buildings may be missing. Zoning limits are simplified. Planned developments count as zero under today's zoning, which understates what's already approved. Very large buildings that span several parcels may be credited to only one of them.
